# Inflorescence types

A flower component's **Type** picks how its flowers are arranged: a single
flower, a raceme, an umbel, a cyme and so on. The list is long, but the types
fall into a few families that grow in genuinely different ways. Knowing the
family tells you which settings will matter.

## Three separate questions

Three settings in three places decide where a plant flowers and how, and they
are easy to confuse:

- **Attachment Location**, on the flower component, says **where** an
  inflorescence starts: at the tips (Terminal), at the nodes (Axillary,
  Internodal, Leaf-opposed), from old wood (Cauliflorous, Ramiflorous), or from
  the ground (Basal).
- **Type**, on the same component, says **what** grows there: one flower, or a
  stalk of many in a given arrangement.
- **Growth Pattern**, on a level, says how the plant's leafy axes carry on.
  It shapes the stems, not the inflorescence. A sympodial stem and a cyme both
  relay, but one is the plant's frame and the other is its flowering head.

## The families

**One flower.** **Solitary** puts a single flower at each place the component
takes. The flower's look is all in its mesh.

**Flowers along a continuing axis.** **Raceme**, **Spike**, **Corymb** and
**Verticillaster** grow a flowering stalk that keeps extending, with flowers
along it, oldest at the bottom. They differ in the details: a raceme's flowers
have stalks (pedicels), a spike's sit on the axis, a corymb's lower stalks are
longer so the flowers reach one level, and a verticillaster puts its flowers in
rings with a pair of bracts under each and bare stem between. The settings that
matter are flower **Spacing**, **Count**, the **Pedicel** fold and **Bloom**
order.

**Flowers from one point.** **Umbel** and **Flat Umbel** radiate every pedicel
from the tip. A flat umbel's pedicels are worked out so every flower lands on
one plane, as in a cow parsley head. Pedicel length and **Spread** shape it.

**Branched heads.** **Panicle** is a branched raceme: the axis carries
branches, and the branches carry the flowers. The compound types, **Compound
Spike**, **Compound Corymb**, **Compound Umbel** and **Compound Flat Umbel**,
repeat their own arrangement one level down, so each flower position holds a
small copy of the whole head. The **Branching** fold (Depth and the branch
settings) matters here.

**Relayed chains.** The cymes end differently from everything above. Their tip
flowers first and stops, and new shoots from below it carry on and flower in
turn. **Scorpioid** and **Helicoid** cymes relay with one shoot, zig-zagging or
spiralling; a **Dichasium** relays with two, forking at every spent flower; a
**Pleiochasium** with a ring of three or more. **Cyme (custom)** exposes the
relay settings directly. A cyme opens in the order it grows: with **Bloom**
order on **Middle first** (what picking a cyme sets), the tip flower opens
first and each relay generation one step later, so a dichasium of depth 3 runs
0, 1/3, 2/3, 1 along the bloom timeline. **Outside first** reverses it.

**Catkins.** **Catkin** is the one type a stem does not turn into. The twigs
go on being twigs and hang catkins on short stalks along their length, as hazel
and willow do.

**None** grows no flowers, for a foliage plant.

## Picking a type sets other settings

Choosing a Type fills several flower settings with values that suit it, such
as the pedicel length, spacing and branching for a compound head. Tune them
after choosing. Changing the Type replaces them, so pick the type first.

The generated **Inflorescence types** table in the reference lists every type
with its description.
